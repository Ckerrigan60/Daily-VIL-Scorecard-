# Client Kickoff Brief — Style Guide

How V.I.L. Landscaping opens a new project with a client. Modeled on the brief David
Gibson (Rockback Design Studio) sends at the start of a job — straight, transparent,
and contacts-forward from the get-go. Use `client-project-brief.md` as the fill-in template.

## The one rule

**The client should finish page one knowing exactly who's involved, what it costs,
what's included, and where things stand today.** No hidden ball, no mystery pricing,
no "we'll figure the team out later." Transparency is the pitch.

## Why each section is there

1. **Header with a real human + direct line.** Name, title, cell, email, website — right
   at the top. A client should be able to phone the owner, not just an inbox. This sets
   the tone: you're reachable and accountable.

2. **"Prepared for [client] · date · timeline."** States who it's for, when it was
   written, and how urgent it is — in one honest line.

3. **Design Direction.** A short vision sentence, then plain-English bullets of what the
   client actually said they want. Proves you listened before proving you can build.

4. **Numbered scope (01–04).** Line items, numbered. Removes ambiguity about what Phase 1
   covers.

5. **Budget, laid bare.** Give the real Phase 1 number *and* name the future phases and
   their rough cost. Framing it as a multi-year investment is honest and sets expectations
   before sticker shock does.

6. **Included vs. To Be Confirmed.** Two columns. Everything in the price on the left,
   every add-on and open question on the right — named openly, not buried. This is the
   single most trust-building move in the document.

7. **Full project team.** Every trade the client will deal with, grouped by discipline,
   with real names, direct phone/email, and each person's exact scope — including
   installation partners and alternates. Clients trust a crew they can see. Share
   collaborators' contact info only with their okay.

8. **Status & Next Steps.** An honest snapshot of today. Words like "pending" and
   "to follow" are features, not weaknesses — they show you're not pretending everything
   is locked when it isn't.

## Voice

- Straight and upfront. Say the number, name the unknowns.
- Plain language over industry jargon.
- Short. Two pages. If it's longer, it's hiding something or over-explaining.
- Confident, not salesy. The transparency does the selling.

## Reference example

The original that inspired this: Rockback Design Studio's **4157 Vermont Cres, Burlington**
project brief (Sept 2026) — one page of vision + scope + budget + deliverables, one page
of full project team + status. That two-page shape is the target.
